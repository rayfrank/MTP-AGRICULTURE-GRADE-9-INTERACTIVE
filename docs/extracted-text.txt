Conserving Animal Feed: Hay
During long and short rainy seasons, there is plenty of forage crops (pastures and fodder crops) for domestic animals in most parts of the country. However, during drought seasons, there is scarcity of forage crops leading to starvation and death of the animals. In pastoralist areas, farmers migrate their animals to faraway places in search of pasture. This sometimes leads to communal conflicts as animals compete to graze on limited pastures. Learning how to conserve excess forage can cushion households from fodder shortages during dry seasons. We will learn how to conserve forage through making hay.
Methods of conserving forage
Forage are plants which grow naturally or are planted for feeding livestock animals. The forage plants which are grazed directly by livestock are called pastures while those which are harvested and then fed to livestock are called fodder crops. We will focus on methods of conserving forage as hay, namely baled hay, stacking hay and standing hay.

Let us learn
Hay refers to forage that has been cut and then dried to about 15 to 20% moisture content. Grasses make good hay. However, the type of forage material should be considered when selecting the forage for hay making. For example, grasses with thin stems are preferred to those with thick stems. Grasses such as napier grass may not make good hay because their thick stems may not lose moisture easily unlike Boma Rhodes grass.

Let us learn
Forage can be preserved as baled hay, stacked stovers or standing hay. Conservation of forage helps to:
a. Provide feed to animals during the dry season.
b. Ensure forage is well distributed throughout the year.
c. Ensure better utilisation of available land.
d. Provide income to households when excess hay is sold.
Baled hay is of higher quality in terms of nutritive value compared to stovers and standing hay. The quality of hay is determined by factors such as:
a. The type of forage which is harvested. For example, grasses with thin stems are of high quality.
b. Stage of harvesting. When grasses are at the flowering stage, the quality of forage is high since nutrients are at high concentration.
c. How well the cut pasture or fodder is dried.
d. Length of the drying period. Prolonged drying lowers the quality of hay since nutrients are lost through breakdown of chlorophyll and carotene. This is shown by bleaching of hay. High moisture content of hay at the time of storage can lead to fermentation and growth of mould, hence loss of nutrients.
e. Weather conditions during harvesting and drying period. There should be no rain.
f. Condition of the storage structure. Hay should be kept in a cool, dry, well ventilated and roofed structure.
g. Inclusion of legume pasture such as lucerne or desmodium in the grass pasture. Legume pastures have more protein than grass pastures.

Integrated Farming
One agricultural enterprise produces waste which can be considered as a resource or input in another agricultural enterprise. For this reason, some households practise a combination of several agricultural enterprises in the same compound in a way that they mutually benefit each other and conserve available resources.

===== Page 21 (1.3 Integrated Farming) =====
Importance of integrated farming
Let us learn
Some of the benefits of integrated farming include:
a) Efficient utilisation of resources and hence reduction of production costs. For example, livestock wastes and fish waste water can be used as manure for crops while crop residues and fodder crops are used as livestock feed.
b) Soil conservation through use of organic manures and recycling of nutrients between crops, livestock and fish. In addition, when trees are grown on land, their roots hold the soil firmly thus preventing soil erosion by water.
c) Promotion of environmental sustainability. For example, the use of chemical fertilisers which pollute soil is reduced when organic manures generated in the farm are used in the garden. Animal waste, crop residues and wood within the farm can be used as sources of energy at the household level thus minimising the use of fossil fuels which pollute our environment.
d) Diversification of income sources throughout the year.
e) Promotion of food security and nutrition. Practising different farming activities reduces the risk of total food production failure.
f) High yields per unit area.
Before practising integrated farming, it is important to consider various factors such as availability of land, labour, resources and skills to manage the various enterprises; suitability of ecological conditions (rainfall, temperature, soil type and other environmental factors) to the proposed farming enterprises and cost of running the various enterprises.

Let us learn
In organic gardening, natural inputs such as organic manures, organic foliar feeds and natural pesticides (bio-pesticides) are used instead of synthetic (chemical) fertilisers and pesticides. Cultural practices are used to ensure good environment for crop growth. Examples of cultural practices include selecting pest and disease resistant crops, use of clean seeds, crop rotation, intercropping, cover cropping, mulching, non-chemical weed control, use of trap crops to control pests, proper disposal of diseased crop residues and good sanitation in the gardens. The principle behind organic gardening is to build healthy soil that ensures healthy crops so that they can resist pests and diseases. Organic gardening promotes human health and food safety and also conserves the environment (water, soil and animals including beneficial insects and wildlife).

Let us learn
The best planting site should be selected in order to grow healthy crops. The planting site should be fertile, well drained and free from chemical contamination. It should also receive adequate sunlight. A seedbed is prepared to remove weeds and loosen the soil to ensure proper soil aeration, easy planting and water infiltration. Turning soil helps to destroy various stages of pests by exposing them to the sun or birds. Any infected plant remains should also be removed from the seedbed and destroyed.
In organic gardening, well rotten organic manures should be incorporated into the soil during seedbed preparation. The organic manures should always be ready during the time of seedbed preparation and planting. In the absence of compost or farm yard manure, green manure can also be used to improve soil fertility. Leguminous plants are planted on a seedbed and when they reach flowering stage, they are ploughed into the soil so that they can decompose to release nutrients. An example of a green manuring plant is Mexican sunflower (tithonia).

===== Page 27 (2.1 Organic Gardening) =====
Mexican sunflower (tithonia)
In case drainage is poor, the seedbed can be raised to prevent crop diseases especially those that affect roots. In places where drainage is good and rainfall is adequate, flat seedbeds are suitable. However, in dry areas, water retention seedbeds such as sunken seedbeds and shallow pits (Zai pits) can be prepared. In case there is shortage of space, innovative container gardening such as multi-storey gardens and suspended gardens may be used.

Making natural pesticides
Natural pesticides are environmentally friendly when compared to synthetic pesticides. The aim is to repel pests so as to keep them away from the crops. We will prepare some natural pesticides (bio pesticides) using locally available materials to control pests and some diseases in our growing crops. The best time to spray is early in the morning or the evening. This helps to avoid disintegration from the sun and to minimise harm to predators and beneficial insects such as bees. A few methods of preparing natural pesticides are provided below.
Using ash brew
The alkaline nature of ash may deter some pests that attack crops. Ash from burnt organic materials such as wood or plant residues is mixed thoroughly with clean water in a ratio of 1:10 in a clean container such as a bucket or drum. This means that for every cup of ash, 10 cups of water are used. The mixture is left to soak for a few days or a week depending on the type of material used to make the ash (soft or hard). Bar soap is grated into small pieces and then added into the mixture. The ratio of the soap to ash should be 1:10. The new mixture is then boiled while stirring for about 30 minutes to enhance melting of the soap. The mixture should then be allowed to cool for 1 to 2 hours. After the ash has settled, the liquid (ash brew) should be poured into another container leaving the settled ash behind. Any remaining ash particles can be filtered out using a fine mesh strainer or cheese cloth. The natural pesticide can be sprayed on the leaves, stems and surrounding soil around the crop where pests have been noticed. It may be reapplied after rain or when its effectiveness decreases.

===== Page 33 (2.1 Organic Gardening) =====
Precautions
Ash containing harmful chemicals should not be used. For example, ash from plastic materials and wood which has been treated and painted.
Ash should not be allowed into the spray bottle or knapsack sprayer as it can clog the nozzle.
One should wear protective gear such as gloves and safety goggles when handling the ash or the ash brew.
Before spraying all the crops, one should spray a small area to ensure that the crops can tolerate the ash brew.
Using hot pepper
500 grams of hot pepper fruits are crushed and then mixed with 5 litres of water. The mixture is boiled for 20 minutes and allowed to cool. After sieving the mixture, 5 litres of water mixed with bar soap is added to the filtrate. The pesticide can be used to control ants, aphids, flies and caterpillars.
Using garlic
10 to 12 cloves of crushed garlic are mixed with 1 litre of water. After settling for 24 hours, the mixture is strained using a piece of cloth. A cup of cooking oil is then added to the filtrate and then sprayed on the crop. A mixture of garlic, onion and water can also be used as a pesticide. The strong smell of the spray repels the pests away from the crops.
Using pyrethrum spray
20 g of pyrethrum powder is mixed with 10 litres of water. Soap may be added to enhance long lasting effects. The mixture is then strained and applied immediately on the pests. In addition, dry pyrethrum flowers may be ground into powder and then dusted over insect pests.
Using baking soda spray
Four teaspoons of baking soda are mixed in one litre of water. The mixture is added to two tablespoons of vegetable oil and two tablespoons of liquid soap and shaken vigorously.
Milk spray
One litre of milk is mixed with one litre of water and then sprayed to control mildew, a fungal disease in some vegetable crops.
Using Sodom apple and Mexican marigold
Fruits of Sodom apple and Mexican marigold leaves are crushed and then soaked in water for 2 weeks. Some liquid soap may be added to assist the mixture to stick on the crops. The pesticide is used to repel aphids and other pests.

Other ways of managing pests and diseases in crops
Field hygiene or sanitation
Seriously infected plants should be uprooted and destroyed to prevent spreading pests and diseases. This practice is called roguing. In addition, clean tools and equipment should be used in the garden to avoid introducing diseases in the garden.
Flooding and irrigation
Flooding minimises pests which hide in the soil, for example, cutworms. Overhead irrigation can help to dislodge aphids from the leaves and drown spider mites. However, the two methods should target the specific pests since they can bring more problems. For example, flooding soil can affect respiration of crop roots and also lead to soil erosion. Overhead irrigation, on the other hand, can increase chances of diseases that affect the crop leaves.
Natural predators
It is important to encourage natural predators of pests in the garden. For example, chameleons, ladybird beetles, spiders, green lacewings, parasitic wasps and praying mantis eat various insect pests that attack crops. It is important to have a variety of plants that provide habitat for such beneficial organisms in an organic garden.
Crop rotation
Crops which are related should be planted in a rotation programme because they are attacked by similar pests and diseases. For example, tomatoes should not be planted in a site where potatoes, eggplant and chillies had been planted. Similarly, watermelon should not be planted where cucumber, pumpkin or butternut had been planted in the previous season.
Companion planting
This refers to close planting of different plants to enhance each other's growth and protect them from pests. For example, vegetables can be planted together with some herbs such as basil, mint and rosemary which serve as pest repellents. Carrots and onions are also good companion crops for repelling onion flies and carrot flies respectively.

===== Page 35 (2.1 Organic Gardening) =====
Weed control
Some weeds harbour a number of pests and disease-causing organisms (fungi, bacteria and viruses) which can easily spread to the crops. Therefore, non-chemical methods of weed control such as mulching, uprooting weeds and tilling land are recommended to suppress weed growth. It is important to avoid tilling land when soil is too wet or when it is raining because diseases can spread fast under such conditions.

Grafting in Plants
Grafting is a technique used in crop production. Grafting is done by joining two parts of closely related plants so that they grow as one plant. When they are joined, the plant is called a grafted plant. The upper part of the graft is called a scion while the lower part is called a rootstock. Grafting can be done on crops such as fruit plants and also on flowers.

Carrying out grafting for various reasons
Grafting may be done in crops such as fruits and also on flowers. On flowers such as rose and bougainvillea, it can be done to produce more than one variety of flower colour. On fruit plants, it can be done to ensure that they mature faster by obtaining the scion from a fast maturing food crop. It can also be done to get improved varieties of fruits, to produce a fruit plant that can resist some diseases or a plant that can tolerate a harsh environment. Grafting can also be done to repair a damaged plant or to rejuvenate (renew growth of) an old plant.

3. Take some clean dry leafy vegetables and arrange them on the tray inside the dryer. Ensure they are arranged in a single layer and evenly spread to allow adequate air circulation.
4. Close the sun dryer's entry (door) to trap the heat inside the sun dryer.
5. Check the drying vegetables after a few hours and turn them to ensure they dry evenly. The drying period may vary from a few days to a week depending on the weather conditions and the amount of moisture in the vegetables.
6. Remove the vegetables from the sun dryer once they are dried and put them in a tightly closed container for preservation. Caution
Observe personal hygiene when handling the vegetables.
Ensure the vegetables put in the sun dryer are clean and free from contamination. When it is very cold, especially at night, put the sun dryer inside a room to protect your vegetables.
